Atoms, shells and periods

The ideas that explain why the table above is shaped the way it is — from what's inside a single atom to why each row behaves the way it does.

What is an atom?

Every element is made of atoms — tiny particles with a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of electrons. The number of protons is the atomic number, and it's what defines the element: change the proton count and you have a different element entirely. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons always equals the number of protons.

Carbon (C), atomic number 6 — 6 protons (orange) and 6 neutrons (grey) packed into the nucleus, with 6 electrons arranged in two shells: 2 in the inner shell, 4 in the outer shell.
Atomic number (Z)Number of protons. Defines the element and its position on the table.
Mass number (A)Protons + neutrons. Shown in the panel as atomic mass, averaged across isotopes.
IsotopesSame element, different neutron count — same chemistry, different mass.

Electron shells

Electrons don't orbit randomly — they occupy shells (energy levels) around the nucleus, filling from the innermost outward. Each shell has a maximum capacity given by 2n², where n is the shell number. This is exactly the data shown in each element's detail panel above.

Shell (n)FormulaMax electronsExample
1 (innermost)2(1)²2Helium fills it completely
22(2)²8Neon fills it completely
32(3)²18Argon stops at 8, full 18 fills later
42(4)²32Only reached by heavier elements

A shell doesn't have to be full before the next one starts gaining electrons — outer shells begin filling once it becomes energetically favorable, which is why potassium (K) puts its 19th electron in a new 4th shell instead of continuing to fill its 3rd.

Valence electrons

The electrons in an atom's outermost shell are called valence electrons, and they're the ones involved in bonding. Atoms react in ways that let them fill or empty that outer shell — usually chasing a stable 8-electron "octet." This single number explains most of chemistry: how many bonds an atom forms, whether it's a metal or nonmetal, and how reactive it is.

This is also why columns (groups) on the periodic table matter so much: every element in group 1 — Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr — has exactly 1 valence electron, so they all react in similar, predictable ways despite having very different sizes.

Periods: rows of the table

A period is a horizontal row. Every element in a period has the same number of electron shells — moving left to right adds protons and electrons to that same outer shell rather than opening a new one. There are 7 periods, with 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, and 32 elements respectively.

Because nuclear charge increases while the shell count stays fixed, four trends run consistently across every period:

Atomic radius
→ shrinks
Ionization energy
→ rises
Electronegativity
→ rises
Metallic character
→ falls

Periods explain size and structure; groups (columns) explain reactivity, since group members share the same valence electron count. Expand each period below for what makes it distinct.

1Period 1 — the shortest row⌄
H · He Only 2 elements, because the first shell holds a maximum of 2 electrons. Hydrogen doesn't fit neatly into any group — it's sometimes shown with the alkali metals, sometimes with the halogens. Helium, despite sharing a column with the alkaline earth metals by electron count, behaves like a noble gas because its single shell is already full.
2Period 2 — the textbook period⌄
Li · Be · B · C · N · O · F · Ne The clearest left-to-right trend on the table: reactive metal (Li) → harder metal (Be) → metalloid-adjacent nonmetal (B) → the backbone-of-life elements (C, N, O) → the most electronegative element that exists (F) → inert gas (Ne). Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen here are central to organic and biological chemistry.
3Period 3 — bigger, same pattern⌄
Na · Mg · Al · Si · P · S · Cl · Ar Chemically mirrors period 2 — sodium behaves like lithium, chlorine behaves like fluorine — but a third shell makes the atoms larger. This period also introduces expanded bonding for elements like S and P, made possible by nearby d-orbitals.
4Period 4 — transition metals begin⌄
K · Ca · Sc → Zn · Ga → Kr The first row to include transition metals (Sc through Zn), where the d-subshell starts filling. This introduces variable oxidation states, colored compounds, and catalytic behavior — think iron, copper and zinc. Trends are gentler here since electrons are filling an inner shell, not the outer one.
5Period 5 — same structure, one size up⌄
Rb · Sr · Y → Cd · In → Xe Structurally identical to period 4 — another 18-element row with a d-block — but everything is a size class bigger. Includes silver, cadmium, tin and iodine. Some expected trends get irregular here (Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh) because the energy gap between subshells narrows at this size.
6Period 6 — the f-block appears⌄
Cs · Ba · La → Lu (lanthanides) · Hf → Rn The first row with an f-block: the lanthanides insert a 4f subshell that fills "underneath" the outer chemistry, which is why lanthanides all behave remarkably alike and are grouped as "rare earths." This period also shows relativistic effects in heavy atoms — part of why gold is gold-colored and mercury is liquid at room temperature.
7Period 7 — radioactive and synthetic⌄
Fr · Ra · Ac → Lr (actinides) · Rf → Og Includes the actinides, all radioactive — uranium and plutonium are the most practically significant. Past uranium (element 92), everything is synthetic, made in reactors or particle accelerators, often atom by atom, decaying within seconds. Chemistry here is largely theoretical, which is why the table labels elements 104+ as "unknown/predicted."

Reactions & the conservation of mass

How atoms rearrange without disappearing — and how to prove it yourself.

Chemical reaction anatomy

Every chemical equation has the same basic parts. Take methane burning in oxygen:

CH4 (g) + 2O2 (g) → CO2 (g) + 2H2O (l)

ReactantsThe substances you start with, written before the arrow — here, CH₄ and O₂.
ProductsThe substances formed, written after the arrow — here, CO₂ and H₂O.
Arrow (→)Means "yields" or "produces." A double arrow (⇌) means the reaction is reversible.
CoefficientThe big number in front (the 2 in 2O₂) — how many units of that substance react. This is what you change to balance an equation.
SubscriptThe small number after an element (the 4 in CH₄) — atoms within one molecule. Changing it makes a different substance, so it's never touched when balancing.
State symbols(s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous — dissolved in water. They describe physical state, not part of the formula.

Ion formation

Atoms form ions by losing or gaining valence electrons to reach a full, stable outer shell — the same drive behind bonding described earlier. Metals lose electrons to form positive ions (cations); nonmetals gain electrons to form negative ions (anions).

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Sodium loses its 1 valence electron, forming a stable cation with a full outer shell.
Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
Magnesium loses 2 valence electrons to reach the same stable configuration.
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Chlorine gains 1 electron, filling its outer shell to become a stable anion.
O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻
Oxygen gains 2 electrons, also reaching a full outer shell.

Opposite charges attract, so a cation and anion pulled together form an ionic bond — e.g. Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl. The electron lost by sodium is exactly the electron gained by chlorine — nothing is created or destroyed, only transferred. That's ion formation's first hint of the bigger law coming next.

Law of Conservation of Mass

First established by Antoine Lavoisier in the 1780s: mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction — only rearranged. The total mass of the reactants always equals the total mass of the products. Atoms aren't gained or lost; they're just regrouped into new substances.

This is exactly why chemical equations must be balanced — the same number of each type of atom has to appear on both sides. An unbalanced equation is really just an incomplete description of the reaction:

Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃
✗ unbalanced — 1 Fe and 2 O on the left, 2 Fe and 3 O on the right
4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃
✓ balanced — 4 Fe and 12 O (6×2) on both sides

If you weighed every atom of iron and oxygen before this reaction and every atom of rust after, the totals would match exactly — that's conservation of mass in action.

Writing chemical equations

Equations are usually built in three stages, moving from a description in words to a precise, balanced formula:

1 · Word equationmethane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
2 · Symbol equation (unbalanced)CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
3 · Balanced symbol equationCH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
How to balance an equation
  1. Count the atoms of each element on both sides.
  2. Add coefficients (whole numbers in front of formulas) to make the counts match — never change a subscript.
  3. Balance one element at a time; leave hydrogen and oxygen until last, since they usually appear in the most compounds.
  4. Recount everything once you're done. If any element is still uneven, adjust and recheck again.

Modeling with Molymod kits

Classroom activity

Molymod (ball-and-stick) kits let you build physical 3D models of the reactants, then take them apart and rebuild them as the products — using exactly the same atoms.

  • Build every reactant molecule first and count each colored atom you used.
  • Break the models apart and reassemble the atoms into the product molecules — don't add or remove any pieces.
  • If you run out of an atom type, or have pieces left over, your equation isn't balanced yet — go back and check your coefficients.
  • This is conservation of mass made physical: the same set of atoms, just rearranged into new bonds.
